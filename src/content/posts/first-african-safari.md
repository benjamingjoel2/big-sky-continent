The engine cuts out and the silence arrives all at once. It is not quite dawn. The canvas sides of the vehicle are rolled up, the air smells of dust and crushed grass, and somewhere out in the grey a francolin is shouting the same three notes over and over. Your guide lifts a hand, not pointing, just asking you to wait. You wrap your fingers round a tin mug of coffee that is cooling far too fast. Then a shape that was a termite mound stands up, stretches, and becomes a lioness.

Nobody forgets that first morning. The good news is that planning it is far less mysterious than it looks from home. Here is what you need to know before you book.

## Where to go: East or southern Africa

For a first safari, most people choose between two broad regions.

**East Africa**, chiefly Kenya and Tanzania, is the classic picture: rolling grassland, flat-topped acacias and enormous skies. This is the country of the Great Migration, the vast movement of wildebeest and zebra around the Serengeti and the Masai Mara, and of famous parks such as Amboseli, Tarangire and the Ngorongoro Crater. If the open plains are what you have been dreaming of, start here. Our guide to [choosing between Kenya and Tanzania](/stories/kenya-or-tanzania-first-safari/) goes park by park.

**Southern Africa** offers different textures. In Botswana there are the channels and islands of the Okavango Delta and the wide spaces of the Kalahari. South Africa has Kruger National Park, where well-made roads and rest camps make self-driving realistic, and private reserves where guided drives are the norm. South Africa is also easy to pair with Cape Town and the Winelands.

There is no wrong answer. Think about what you most want to see and feel, how much time you have, and whether you would rather drive yourself or be guided.

## When to go: dry season and green season

Safari seasons are less about temperature than about water.

In the **dry season**, grass dies back, waterholes shrink and animals gather near rivers and permanent water, which makes them easier to find. Tanzania National Parks describes Tarangire, for example, as the place where large herds of elephant, wildebeest, zebra and buffalo [gather in the dry season](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/en/tarangire). For the Serengeti, the park authority gives [June to October as the dry season](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/en/serengeti) for the Migration, and January to February as calving season.

The **green season** brings rain, fresh grass, newborn animals, migrant birds and fewer vehicles. In Kenya, the Meteorological Department describes March to May as the main ["Long Rains" season and October to December as the "Short Rains"](https://meteo.go.ke/documents/2537/March-April-May_MAM_2026_Seasonal_Weather_Forecast.pdf). Rain can make tracks muddy; the UK Foreign Office warns that dirt roads in Tanzania's parks can become [hazardous or impassable after heavy rain](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/safety-and-security). But green-season days often mix sunshine with short storms, and the landscapes are at their most beautiful.

The dry months do not fall at the same time everywhere on the continent, so check the pattern for your particular park. If the Migration is your goal, our [Great Migration month by month](/stories/great-migration-month-by-month/) guide explains where the herds tend to be through the year.

## Types of safari

The word "safari" covers a surprising range of trips.

- **Lodge safaris.** Solid rooms, a restaurant, often a pool. Comfortable and a gentle introduction if you are nervous about sleeping under canvas.
- **Tented camps.** Proper beds and en suite bathrooms inside large canvas tents. You hear everything at night, which is half the magic.
- **Mobile safaris.** Camps that move with the season, or simple camps put up for a few nights at a time. Closer to the old idea of a safari.
- **Self-drive.** Hiring a vehicle and exploring on your own. It works best in parks with good roads and clear rules, Kruger being the classic example. It is less suited to remote areas.
- **Fly-in safaris.** Light aircraft hop between airstrips, saving long days on the road. Tanzania National Parks notes [regular flights to Seronera airstrip](https://tanzaniaparks.go.tz/serengeti/visitor-guide) in the Serengeti, while the road journey can take seven to eight hours. Nyerere National Park can be reached by [flights from Dar es Salaam](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/en/nyerere) to park airstrips. If you charter a plane in Kenya, the Foreign Office advises choosing a company whose crew includes [a safety pilot qualified to take over the controls](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/safety-and-security).
- **Walking safaris.** On foot with an armed ranger or guide, reading tracks and noticing the small things. Ruaha lists [walking safaris among its activities](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/en/ruaha), and in the Ngorongoro Conservation Area walks require [an armed ranger](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/visitor-guidelines-and-procedures/). Not every area allows them. The Maasai Mara National Reserve's management plan, for instance, says walking safaris and night drives [are not permitted inside the reserve](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/MNNR-Management-Plan-Final-February-2023-1_compressed.pdf), while some of the surrounding [community conservancies offer walking](https://maraconservancies.org/mara-conservancies/).

Many first trips mix two or three of these: a couple of nights in a lodge, a tented camp in the heart of the action, and a short flight in between.

## What a safari day looks like

Safari days follow the animals, and the animals follow the sun.

You will usually be woken before dawn, perhaps with a knock and a "good morning" outside the tent. Tea or coffee, a rusk or biscuit, and you are out as the light comes up. Early morning is when predators are still active and the air is cool. Gate and driving times are set by the parks: in the Ngorongoro Conservation Area, driving is permitted [from 06:00 to 18:00](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/visitor-guidelines-and-procedures/), and Serengeti gates [close at 6pm](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/en/serengeti).

By late morning the heat builds and most animals find shade. So do you. Brunch or lunch, a siesta, a book on the veranda, the swimming pool if there is one. This is not wasted time. You will be glad of it.

Mid-afternoon you head out again, as the light softens and the animals stir. Then comes the sundowner: a stop somewhere with a view, a drink in hand, the sky turning from gold to pink to ink. You drive back in the last of the light, and the evening ends around a fire, swapping stories of what you saw.

## What to pack

Pack light and soft. Light aircraft have strict luggage limits, so ask your operator for the exact allowance before you buy a suitcase.

- **Neutral clothes** in layers: early mornings in an open vehicle can be properly cold, and middays hot.
- **A warm fleece or light jacket**, a sun hat and a scarf or buff for dust.
- **Long sleeves and trousers** for evenings, when mosquitoes are about.
- **Comfortable closed shoes**, especially if you plan to walk.
- **Binoculars**, ideally one pair each. They transform a safari.
- **A camera with a zoom lens** and spare batteries; charging may be limited in remote camps.
- **Sunscreen, insect repellent, a head torch** and any medicines you need, in their original packaging.
- **Reusable bags rather than plastic.** The Ngorongoro Conservation Area, for example, asks visitors [not to bring plastic bags](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/visitor-guidelines-and-procedures/) into the area.

## How guides and park rules work

Your guide is the heart of a safari. A good one reads the land like a newspaper, knows when to wait and when to move on, and keeps you safe. Listen to them, and ask questions; most guides love sharing what they know.

Park rules exist to protect both you and the wildlife, and they are broadly similar across the region:

- **Stay in the vehicle** unless you are in a designated area. The Ngorongoro Conservation Area Authority requires visitors to [remain in their vehicles at all times](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/visitor-guidelines-and-procedures/) except in designated areas, and the Serengeti's [Mara River crossing guideline](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1724693797-MararivercrossingGuideline.pdf) says the same. In South Africa, Kruger's communications general manager has reminded visitors that SANParks regulations say they [must remain inside their vehicles](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2026-04-09-kruger-visitor-incident-sparks-warning-why-getting-out-of-your-car-can-get-you-killed/), and that feeding animals, loud noise and off-road driving are prohibited.
- **Never feed animals.** Tanzania National Parks asks Serengeti visitors to [avoid feeding wildlife](https://tanzaniaparks.go.tz/serengeti/visitor-guide), and the CDC's health advice is also clear: [do not touch or feed animals](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya) you don't know.
- **Keep your distance and keep quiet.** The Ngorongoro guidelines ask for at least 25 metres between you and animals, no more than five vehicles at a sighting, and silence while you watch. The Maasai Mara management plan sets out [similar limits for big-cat sightings](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/MNNR-Management-Plan-Final-February-2023-1_compressed.pdf) in parts of the reserve.
- **Stay on the tracks** where off-road driving is not allowed, and respect speed limits.
- **No drones** without permission: the Serengeti visitor guide says they are [prohibited without special permission](https://tanzaniaparks.go.tz/serengeti/visitor-guide).

The Foreign Office sums it up for both Kenya and Tanzania: [always follow park regulations and advice from wardens](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/safety-and-security).

## Health and entry: the short version

This is not the place for the full detail, so here are the essentials, all checked November 2026. Please re-check before you travel, because rules change and depend on your nationality.

**Health.** See a travel health professional early. TravelHealthPro suggests [four to six weeks before you travel](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/115/kenya) where possible. Malaria is a risk in many safari areas: the CDC notes that in Kenya transmission occurs [in all areas below 2,500 metres, including game parks](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya), and recommends antimalarials for much of [Tanzania](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/tanzania). Our guide to [malaria, vaccines and staying healthy in Africa](/stories/malaria-vaccines-health-africa/) covers this properly.

**Entry.** For British passport holders (checked November 2026):

- **Kenya** requires an [Electronic Travel Authorisation](https://www.etakenya.go.ke/) before you travel, and the Foreign Office says the authorities recommend applying [at least two weeks ahead](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/entry-requirements). From 5 October 2026, most visitors also need Kenyan government-provided travel health insurance, bought during the eTA application.
- **Tanzania** requires a visa; the Foreign Office points to the official e-visa system and notes that most visitors also need [mandatory Tanzanian government-provided inbound travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/entry-requirements).
- **Botswana** allows British visitors up to 90 days without a visa, but your passport needs [at least three blank pages](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/botswana/entry-requirements), and children need birth certificates.
- **South Africa** allows British visitors up to 90 days without a visa; your passport needs [two blank pages](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/south-africa/entry-requirements), and there are special rules for children.

If you hold a different passport, check your own government's advice and the destination's official immigration site.

## How to choose an operator

A safari is only as good as the people running it. The Foreign Office advises using [reputable tour operators](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/safety-and-security) and never buying safari tours from touts. Before you book, ask:

- **Are you licensed?** In Kenya, the Tourism Regulatory Authority licenses tourism businesses and offers [an online way to verify a licence](https://www.tra.go.ke/). In Tanzania, the Foreign Office notes that the Tanzanian Association of Tour Operators [keeps a list of licensed companies](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/safety-and-security).
- **Who will guide me, and how many guests share a vehicle?** Ask about guide qualifications and experience, whether you are guaranteed a window seat, and whether a private vehicle is an option.
- **What exactly is included?** Park and conservation fees, transfers, flights, meals, drinks and activities vary hugely between quotes. Ask for a written list.
- **What happens if something goes wrong?** Ask about emergency procedures, communications in remote areas and medical evacuation, and check your travel insurance covers it.
- **How do you work with local communities and conservation?** A good operator will be glad to tell you.

For a sense of how the costs add up, read [how much a safari costs](/stories/how-much-does-a-safari-cost/). Then pick the trip that excites you most. The lioness will be waiting.
