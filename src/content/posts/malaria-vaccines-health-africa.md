The light goes quickly. One minute the reed beds are gold, the next they are a soft grey line against a sky turning apricot, then violet. Someone lights the lamps along the path to your tent. The frogs start up, a hundred tiny bells, and beneath them comes a thinner, higher sound: the hum of insects finding the warm air above the water. You pull on a long-sleeved shirt, tuck your trousers into your socks, and reach for the repellent before you walk to supper.

That small ritual at dusk is most of what staying healthy on safari looks like: not fear, just habit. This guide covers the habits and the paperwork.

It is general information, not medical advice. Your health, medicines, route and season all change the answer, so book a proper travel health appointment. Last checked November 2026. Official advice changes, so re-check the linked pages before you travel.

## Start with a travel health appointment

The NHS says you should [try to get advice from a travel health professional at least 4 to 6 weeks before you travel](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/travel-vaccinations/) (checked November 2026). In the US, the CDC's country pages suggest you [visit your doctor at least a month before your trip](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/tanzania). The UK government's travel advice goes further and suggests looking at vaccine recommendations [at least 8 weeks before your trip](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/health). Earlier gives you more options. If time is short, still go.

Take your full itinerary: every country, park and connection, your dates, and anything adventurous such as a mountain climb or a lake stay.

## Malaria: the ABCD

Malaria is the big one to understand. The [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria) says it mostly spreads through the bites of some infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes, that it is preventable and curable, and that in 2024 the WHO African Region was home to about 95 per cent of all malaria cases and deaths (checked November 2026).

UK guidance sums up prevention as the ABCD. As [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/52/malaria) puts it:

- **Awareness** of the risk.
- **Bite prevention.**
- **Chemoprophylaxis**: the right antimalarial tablets, taken exactly as prescribed.
- **Diagnosis**: prompt diagnosis and treatment without delay.

### Risk is not the same everywhere

Malaria risk varies by country, by region within a country, by altitude and by season. The same safari can need tablets in one park and not the next. A few examples from the CDC country pages (checked November 2026):

- **Tanzania**: transmission in [all areas below 1,800 metres](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/tanzania), and CDC recommends prescription medicine for certain areas.
- **Kenya**: transmission in [all areas, including game parks, below 2,500 metres](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya); in the highly urbanised centre of Nairobi transmission is rare and CDC recommends bite precautions rather than tablets.
- **South Africa**: transmission is listed along the borders with Mozambique and Zimbabwe, in parts of KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and Mpumalanga, and in [Kruger National Park](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/south-africa), where tablets are recommended; in some other listed areas CDC advises bite precautions only.
- **Botswana**: transmission districts include [Chobe and the Okavango](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/botswana), with only rare or sporadic cases in some southern districts and none in Gaborone.
- **Namibia**: transmission in several northern regions, including Kavango and Zambezi, rare elsewhere, and [none in Windhoek](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/namibia).

So a Cape Town and Winelands holiday is a very different conversation from a week in the Okavango. Your clinic will weigh up your route, health and other medicines before advising on tablets.

### Bite prevention, every evening

TravelHealthPro notes that some mosquitoes, including *Anopheles*, are [more active from dusk to dawn](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/insect-and-tick-bite-avoidance). Its advice (checked November 2026) includes:

- Use a repellent containing **50 per cent DEET**, which it recommends for all travellers over two months, and reapply it regularly. If DEET does not suit you, ask your clinic about alternatives.
- If you need sunscreen too, put the sunscreen on first and the DEET after.
- Wear loose clothing with long sleeves and long trousers, tucked into socks.
- Sleep under a net where your room is not screened; nets that are not long-lasting need re-treating with insecticide every six to twelve months.

### A fever is urgent, even after you are home

The WHO says malaria symptoms [usually start within 10 to 15 days](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria) of an infected bite, but UK guidance is wider: seek immediate medical attention for possible malaria symptoms [while abroad or up to a year after you return](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/52/malaria), and take any fever seriously. Tell the doctor where you have been.

## Yellow fever and the certificate at the border

Yellow fever is a mosquito-borne virus, and the [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/yellow-fever) classes 27 African countries as high-risk for outbreaks. It also notes that a single dose of vaccine gives lifelong protection (checked November 2026).

There are two separate questions, and it helps to keep them apart:

1. **Do you need the vaccine to protect yourself?** That depends on where you go. CDC recommends it for most travellers to [Kenya](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya), for example, but generally not for travel limited to Nairobi city or most of the coast.
2. **Do you need a certificate to cross a border?** Under the International Health Regulations, countries may require proof of vaccination on an International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis, often called the yellow card. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/disease/194/yellow-fever) keeps country-by-country requirements, and the [WHO country list](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/countries-with-risk-of-yellow-fever-transmission-and-countries-requiring-yellow-fever-vaccination-(november-2022)) sets out risk areas and requirements.

Many countries ask for a certificate only if you arrive from a country with yellow fever risk. CDC lists this rule for [Tanzania](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/tanzania), [South Africa](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/south-africa) and [Botswana](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/botswana): a certificate is required for travellers aged one and over coming from a risk country, and that includes airport transits or layovers of more than 12 hours there (checked November 2026). That matters for multi-country trips and for flights that connect through a risk country, so show your clinic the whole route, including connections.

On the certificate itself, the [CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/page/icvp) says (checked November 2026):

- It becomes valid **10 days after** the date of vaccination, so do not leave it to the last week.
- It is valid for life, even if an older card shows an expiry date.
- It must carry the official stamp of the centre that gave the vaccine.
- Travellers without a valid certificate may be refused entry, quarantined or offered vaccination at the border.

If vaccination is not right for you medically, ask your clinic about an exemption letter and check how your destination treats one before you go. Keep the certificate with your passport.

## Other vaccines your clinic may mention

Some vaccines are commonly advised for many travellers to sub-Saharan Africa, but your clinic will advise on what you need. On its Tanzania and Kenya pages, the CDC asks you to make sure routine vaccines, including measles, are up to date, describes typhoid as recommended for most travellers, and recommends hepatitis A and hepatitis B for many unvaccinated travellers (checked November 2026). The NHS lists [hepatitis A, typhoid, cholera and polio (given as a combined vaccine with tetanus and diphtheria)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/travel-vaccinations/) among travel vaccines free on the NHS after a risk assessment, while vaccines such as rabies and yellow fever are usually paid for.

## Animals, water and food

### Rabies

The CDC notes that [dogs infected with rabies are commonly found in Kenya](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya), that some wildlife can carry it too, and that rabies vaccines after a bite may only be available in larger towns. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/rabies) advises avoiding contact with wild or domestic animals, including bats, during travel. If you are bitten, scratched or licked on broken skin, wash the wound thoroughly with soap or detergent and water and get urgent medical help, even if it seems trivial (checked November 2026). Ask your clinic whether a pre-exposure course suits your trip, especially if you will be far from hospitals.

### Freshwater and schistosomiasis

The lakes are beautiful, and some carry a parasite. Schistosomiasis, also called bilharzia, spreads in fresh water, and the CDC lists it in [Tanzania](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/tanzania) and [Malawi](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/malawi), advising travellers to avoid swimming in fresh, unchlorinated water such as lakes, ponds and rivers. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/28/schistosomiasis) notes that many cases diagnosed in the UK come from swimming in Lake Malawi, and that repellent or towel-drying does not prevent infection (checked November 2026). If you have been in fresh water in a risk area, tell your GP or a travel clinic when you get home; screening is usually done several weeks after exposure.

### Food and water

[TravelHealthPro's food and water advice](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/food-and-water-hygiene) is simple and sensible: wash your hands with soap and clean water, choose freshly prepared, thoroughly cooked food served piping hot, peel or cook fruit and vegetables where possible, rely on treated, filtered or bottled water, and avoid ice (checked November 2026). Get medical advice sooner if you cannot keep fluids down or show signs of dehydration.

## Sun and altitude

African sun is strong, even through cloud on an open vehicle. A wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses, midday shade and plenty of safe water go a long way. If you are wearing both sunscreen and repellent, remember the order: sunscreen first, then DEET.

If Kilimanjaro is on your list, read about altitude before you choose a route. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/26/altitude-illness) says altitude illness usually occurs above 2,500 metres, names Kilimanjaro's 5,895-metre summit as a popular high-altitude destination, and flags ascents of the mountain in less than seven days as a concern. Its key advice is gradual ascent with rest days and descending if symptoms worsen or are severe (checked November 2026).

## Insurance with medical evacuation

A long drive or a short flight separates many camps from a hospital. The UK government's Tanzania advice tells you to check you have [appropriate travel insurance for local treatment or unexpected medical evacuation](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/health). Its [travel insurance guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance) adds that failing to declare a health condition may invalidate your cover, and that you should check every activity you plan is covered (checked November 2026).

## Entry documents: the health paperwork and the rest

Health and borders meet at the passport desk. Alongside a yellow fever certificate where one applies, check:

- **Visas and travel authorisations.** Kenya asks visitors to [apply online for an Electronic Travel Authorisation](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/entry-requirements) before travel, through the [official eTA portal](https://www.etakenya.go.ke/en), and GOV.UK notes the authorities recommend applying at least two weeks ahead. GOV.UK says you [must have a visa to enter Tanzania](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania/entry-requirements) and points to the official e-visa site (checked November 2026).
- **Passport validity and blank pages.** Both Kenya and Tanzania ask for at least six months' validity from arrival, according to GOV.UK.
- **Your government's country page.** UK travellers should read the [FCDO foreign travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice) for every country on the route, including its entry requirements and health sections. US travellers should check the State Department's country information pages and the CDC destination pages.

Rules change, so re-check them shortly before you fly.

## Back to the lamps

By the time the lamps are lit, the work is done: the appointment, the tablets if you need them, the certificate in your passport, the long sleeves. What is left is the evening itself, and the frogs, and the stars. If you are planning a first safari, our guide to [your first African safari](/stories/first-african-safari/) is a good next read.
