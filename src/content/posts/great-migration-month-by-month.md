The engine is off. Someone in the vehicle whispers, then thinks better of it. Below you, on the far bank of the Mara River, a long dark line of wildebeest has been building for what feels like an age: heads down, then up, a few animals edging to the lip of the bank and stepping back again. Dust hangs in the air. The water is the colour of milky tea, and somewhere in it, very still, are the crocodiles. Nobody knows whether the herd will go in five minutes, in an hour or not at all today. Then one animal jumps, and the whole line pours down the bank behind it.

That moment is what most people picture when they hear "the Great Migration". But the river crossings are only one chapter of a story that runs all year, across two countries and one connected ecosystem. Understanding the shape of the year is the single most useful thing you can do before you book.

## First, understand the rain

The Serengeti and Kenya's Maasai Mara are not two separate wildlife areas that happen to share some animals. Tanzania National Parks describes the Serengeti as the core of the "Serengeti-Mara Ecosystem", an area [roughly defined by the annual wildebeest migration](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1634493627-SE-ENG.pdf) that takes in Serengeti National Park, part of the Ngorongoro Conservation Area, several Tanzanian game reserves and the Maasai Mara National Reserve in Kenya. The Frankfurt Zoological Society, a conservation organisation that supports the park, describes the migration as [over two million wildebeest, zebra and gazelle](https://fzs.org/en/programs/tanzania/serengeti-national-park/).

What moves them is grass and water, and what grows the grass is rain. The same TANAPA brochure describes the Serengeti as having a rainy season from November to April and a dry season from May to October. The herds follow fresh grazing, and the rains do not arrive on a fixed date. Narok County's plan for the Greater Mara notes that [rainfall patterns in the Mara have become increasingly erratic](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/GMME-Management-Plan-200320238_compressed.pdf), with droughts becoming longer and more severe.

So read everything below as "where the herds usually are", not as a timetable. A late rain can hold them in one place for weeks; an early one can send them moving before anyone expected. Anyone who promises you a crossing on a particular day is guessing.

## December to March: calving on the southern plains

Around the turn of the year, the herds arrive on the short-grass plains of the southern Serengeti. The Ngorongoro Conservation Area Authority says the best period at Ndutu, on the southern edge of the Serengeti, [starts at the end of December](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/ndutu-plains/) when the herds come down from the north, and runs until April. It describes the area as a calving ground for the migration, with thousands of calves born there during the season.

TANAPA puts calving [between January and March](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1771310361-TANAPA%20Tourism%20Splash_Issue%20no%202%202026.pdf), centred on the Ndutu area and the surrounding short-grass plains, where fresh rains wake the grasses. Births are concentrated into a short window, which in TANAPA's words overwhelms predators such as lions, hyenas and cheetahs.

Picture it: wide, green, open country, the herds spread out to the horizon, newborn calves finding their legs within minutes, and predators never far away. It is a quieter, more intimate side of the migration than the river crossings, and no less gripping.

Ndutu sits across the boundary between Serengeti National Park and the Ngorongoro Conservation Area, so this is also the season when a migration trip combines most naturally with a visit to Ngorongoro Crater.

## April and May: the rains and the move west

As the wet season goes on, the herds start to move. The NCAA describes the animals beginning their journey [northwards again in April](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/ndutu-plains/). This is a wetter, greener and often quieter time to be in the park, and the herds can be harder to pin down as they spread out and move.

By May, TANAPA says, the rut [fills the western corridor](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1759757684-TourismSplash.pdf) with energy: males bellowing, chasing and defending temporary territories. The western side of the park is also where the herds meet the Grumeti River. TANAPA names the crossings of the Grumeti and the Mara as the most dramatic moments of the whole year.

## June to October: the north and the Mara River

From around mid-year the herds push into the northern Serengeti and across the border into Kenya. The Mara Conservancy, writing on its Mara Triangle website, says the migration [usually starts somewhere in June or July and goes on until November](https://www.maratriangle.org/maratriangle/wildebeest-migration). Narok County's ecosystem plan says the migrants use the Greater Mara as their [dry season range between July and November](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/GMME-Management-Plan-200320238_compressed.pdf), spending the rest of the year in Tanzania.

This is the season of the Mara River crossings, and it is when the northern Serengeti and the Maasai Mara are at their busiest. The Mara Conservancy is honest about the hardest part: crossings happen often during the migration but are difficult to predict. Herds can gather at the bank for hours and then turn away. Patience is the whole game. Bring water, a hat, something to read, and the willingness to sit.

It is also worth knowing that the Mara is not only a stage for Serengeti animals. The Mara Conservancy notes that the Mara once had its own wildebeest population that migrated from the Loita Plains, and Narok County's plan describes how fencing and farming have since closed much of that route. When you visit, you are seeing a system under pressure as well as a spectacle.

## November and December: the drift south

As the rains return, the herds leave Kenya and drift back down through the Serengeti towards the southern plains, arriving, as the NCAA puts it, around the end of December, and the cycle begins again. This in-between season can be rewarding if you are flexible: the landscape is greening, and the herds are on the move rather than settled in one place. It is also the hardest time to predict, so build some slack into your plans.

## Where to base yourself, season by season

Because the herds move across a very large area, where you sleep matters more than almost anything else. As a rough guide:

- **Late December to March:** the southern Serengeti and the Ndutu area of the Ngorongoro Conservation Area, for calving.
- **April and May:** the wet season, with herds moving north and west. A flexible itinerary that can follow them helps.
- **May into early summer:** the western corridor and the Grumeti River.
- **Roughly July to October:** the northern Serengeti on the Tanzanian side, or the Maasai Mara and its surrounding conservancies in Kenya, for the river crossings.
- **November and December:** the herds heading south again, so central and southern areas.

Remember that the herds and the boundaries do not line up neatly. If your heart is set on crossings, staying close to the river gives you more chances to be there when it happens, rather than spending hours driving to reach it. ## Booking ahead in the busy months

The crossing season is the peak time in both the northern Serengeti and the Mara. Narok County's reserve management plan refers to the [tourism high season when wildebeest crossings are taking place](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/MNNR-Management-Plan-Final-February-2023-1_compressed.pdf). If you want those months, plan early rather than hoping to find space at the last minute. Calving season in the south draws visitors too, so the same advice applies.

The quieter months have their own rewards: fewer vehicles, greener landscapes and resident wildlife that does not migrate at all. The NCAA points out that Ndutu has resident wildlife year-round, so it is worth visiting outside the calving season too.

## Crossings, crowds and how to behave

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the crossings: vehicles can get in the way. Narok County's management plan for the Maasai Mara National Reserve says that [more than 150 vehicles have sometimes been recorded at a single crossing](https://narok.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/MNNR-Management-Plan-Final-February-2023-1_compressed.pdf), and that crowding has in the past interrupted crossings or led to large numbers of wildebeest dying.

The reserve's visitor code of conduct, set out in that plan, is clear. At migration river crossings, do not block or disturb crossings by parking too close or driving through herds, and never get out of your vehicle at or around crossing areas, which it calls extremely dangerous. More widely in the reserve, keep at least 25 metres from animals whenever possible; a maximum of five vehicles are allowed around lions, rhinos, leopards and cheetahs, and if five are already there you should wait at least 100 metres away; when others are waiting, move on after 15 minutes. Off-road driving is not allowed in the Mara River zone. Talk quietly, and do not clap, shout or cheer. Rangers can fine you, remove you or ban you from the reserve if the rules are broken.

On the Tanzanian side, TANAPA says the Serengeti has introduced [strict rules and designated viewing areas at the crossings](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1759757684-TourismSplash.pdf): vehicles must stay stationary and silent, herds must be given space, and visitors must stay inside vehicles at all times. The park's general rules include a 50 km/h speed limit, no driving between 7pm and 6am, and staying on roads and tracks except where off-road driving is specifically allowed, as the [Serengeti brochure](https://www.tanzaniaparks.go.tz/uploads/publications/en-1634493627-SE-ENG.pdf) sets out.

In practice, good etiquette is mostly about your guide, and about you backing your guide when they choose to hang back. A vehicle parked well away from the bank, engine off, is the one most likely to see a crossing happen at all. If you feel a driver is pushing in too close, it is fine to say so.

## Ngorongoro, next door

The Ngorongoro Conservation Area wraps around the south-eastern side of the Serengeti, and it is a different kind of protected area. The NCAA describes the crater as the [world's largest intact and unfilled volcanic caldera](https://www.ncaa.go.tz/ngorongoro-crater/), with its floor sitting roughly 610 metres below the rim, and says the name comes from a Maasai phrase meaning "big bowl". Only four-wheel-drive vehicles may enter the crater, and walking is not allowed on the crater floor, though it is permitted on the rim and in other parts of the conservation area.

If you visit during calving season, the Ndutu plains and the crater sit comfortably in the same trip. At other times of year, Ngorongoro is a natural first or last stop on the way to or from the Serengeti.

## The short version

The herds spend roughly the first months of the year calving in the south, move north and west as the wet season goes on, reach the Grumeti and then the Mara River in the dry months, and drift south again as the rains return. Rain sets the timing, and it changes every year. Choose your season for the experience you want, base yourself where the herds are likely to be, book early for the crossing months, and when the line of wildebeest finally tips over the bank, make sure your vehicle is not the one standing in its way.
