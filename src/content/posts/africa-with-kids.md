It is late afternoon and the car has gone quiet. Not the sulky quiet of a long drive, but the held-breath kind. Ahead, a line of zebras is picking its way down to the water, stopping, starting, ears turning. A small voice in the back seat whispers "there's another one" and then counts, under its breath, all the way to twenty-three. Nobody has mentioned a screen for an hour.

That is the thing about travelling in Africa with children: the big moments are genuinely big, and children feel them without needing anything explained. The trick is choosing places where the practical side, from health to long drives to paperwork, does not swallow the magic. Here are the trips that tend to work best for families, and the details worth getting right before you book.

## Start with a low-risk malaria safari in South Africa

For many families, malaria is the first question, and South Africa has the easiest answer. The UK's National Travel Health Network and Centre puts the risk in the low-altitude areas of Mpumalanga and Limpopo, including Kruger National Park, with a lower risk in the far northeast of KwaZulu-Natal, and says there is "no risk in the rest of South Africa" ([TravelHealthPro, checked November 2026](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/south-africa)). The US CDC lists much the same transmission areas ([CDC, checked November 2026](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/south-africa)). Both still recommend avoiding bites everywhere, so pack repellent and long sleeves for the evenings.

That leaves you plenty of wildlife country outside the risk areas. South African Tourism names Madikwe and Pilanesberg in North West province among its malaria-free family choices ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/cd/en/travel/article/a-safari-adventure-for-the-whole-family)). Madikwe sits right up against the Botswana border and is described as one of the country's foremost Big Five malaria-free reserves ([South African Tourism](https://southafrica.net/gh/en/travel/article/turning-farmland-back-to-nature-at-madikwe-game-reserve-north-west)). Pilanesberg spreads across the floor of an ancient, long-extinct volcano and holds all of the Big Five; the nearby town of Rustenburg is about 150km from Johannesburg ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/gl/en/travel/article/rustenburg-north-west)). That relatively short transfer from a major airport matters a great deal when your travellers are small.

The Eastern Cape is the other big option. South African Tourism pitches its malaria-free safaris as an experience "just as exciting" as Kruger, and Addo Elephant National Park is the headline: elephants nearly wiped out by hunting now flourish there ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/gl/en/travel/article/eastern-cape-safaris-see-the-big-5-and-much-more)). Children also tend to love Addo's flightless dung beetle, which the same page says is found only in the park.

Re-check the malaria maps for your exact route before you travel, and talk to a travel health clinic. Our guide to [malaria, vaccines and health in Africa](/stories/malaria-vaccines-health-africa/) explains how to prepare.

## Cape Town and the Garden Route: the gentle introduction

If your children are very young, or you simply want a trip with beaches, ice creams and wildlife in easy measure, Cape Town and the Garden Route are hard to beat. South Africa's Western Cape sits outside the malaria risk areas described above, and the days can be shaped around naps and appetites.

At Boulders Beach in Simon's Town, boardwalks lead you across the beaches to watch the African penguins, and South African Tourism describes the beach itself as "an ideal swimming spot for children" ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/gl/en/travel/article/visit-the-boulders-beach-penguins)). Watching a penguin waddle past a sandcastle is the kind of thing children remember for years. Keep a respectful distance; these are wild birds.

Further east, the Garden Route is made for road trips in short hops. Tsitsikamma forms part of the greater Garden Route National Park, and you can walk through ancient forest to a huge yellowwood known simply as "The Big Tree" ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/gl/en/travel/article/tsitsikamma-a-coastal-adventure-you-ll-never-forget)). The Garden Route also connects neatly to the Eastern Cape, so you can finish a coastal drive with a malaria-free safari.

General safety advice for South Africa is on the FCDO's page, which was updated in early November 2026 with information on scams, taxis and ride-hailing apps ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/south-africa)).

## Paperwork for children in South Africa

This is the part families most often get wrong. The FCDO says there are special requirements for travelling to South Africa with children aged 17 and under, and directs you to the Department of Home Affairs for the details ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/south-africa/entry-requirements)). Home Affairs' own leaflet on travelling with children says the rules apply to foreign and South African travellers, on departure as well as arrival. It asks parents travelling with a child for a full ("unabridged") birth certificate showing the parents' details, and asks a parent travelling alone with a child for an affidavit of consent from the other parent ([Department of Home Affairs](https://www.dha.gov.za/files/Brochures/Immigrationleaflet.pdf)).

The leaflet dates from when the rules came into force, and the requirements have been revised since, so do not rely on it alone. Check the current position with Home Affairs or your nearest South African mission well before you fly. If in doubt, carry the documents anyway: a full birth certificate weighs nothing, and being turned away at check-in ruins a holiday.

## Namibia: a self-drive with floodlit waterholes

Namibia is a superb family road trip, with wide skies, very little traffic and the thrill of finding animals for yourself. Etosha National Park is the centrepiece: a park dominated by an expansive salt pan, whose waterholes, the park authority says, "are famous among international tourists for spectacular game viewing" ([Ministry of Environment, Forestry and Tourism](https://www.meft.gov.na/national-parks/etosha-national-park/217/)).

The best part for children comes after dark. The state-run Okaukuejo rest camp, 17km from the park's southern entrance, is "most famous for its floodlit waterhole", where you can watch wildlife at close quarters day and night ([Namibia Wildlife Resorts](https://www.nwr.com.na/resorts/okaukuejo-resort/)). The park authority mentions black rhino, lion and elephant at night ([Ministry of Environment, Forestry and Tourism](https://www.meft.gov.na/national-parks/etosha-national-park/217/)). Sitting on a bench in your pyjamas while a rhino drinks in the light is about as good as family travel gets.

Two practical points. First, malaria: TravelHealthPro lists a high risk from December to April in several northern regions, including Kunene, Oshana and Oshikoto, and a low risk there from May to November ([TravelHealthPro, checked November 2026](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/namibia)); CDC lists similar regions ([CDC, checked November 2026](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/namibia)). Check the regional breakdown against your route and dates with a travel clinic.

Second, paperwork. The FCDO says parents travelling with children aged 17 and under must carry each child's passport and a full birth certificate with both parents' details, and that an abridged birth certificate will not be accepted. UK nationals have also needed a visa for Namibia since 1 April 2025 ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/namibia/entry-requirements)).

Distances are long, so plan short driving days with stops. The FCDO warns that accidents are common on gravel and dirt roads and advises against driving outside towns after dark because wildlife and livestock are serious hazards ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/namibia/safety-and-security)). With children, that rule makes life easier anyway: arrive by mid-afternoon, swim, then head to the waterhole.

## Kenya, with a beach to finish

Kenya gives older children the classic safari picture: open savannah, big herds and the chance to watch predators at work. Pair it with a few days on the Indian Ocean coast and you have a trip that balances early starts with long, lazy afternoons. Our guide to [safari and beach combinations](/stories/safari-and-beach-combinations/) goes into the options.

Be clear-eyed about health. The CDC lists malaria risk in all areas of Kenya below 2,500m, including game parks ([CDC, checked November 2026](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/kenya)), so this is a trip that needs antimalarials, and that brings in questions of age and weight. The CDC notes, for example, that doxycycline is contraindicated in children under eight, and that doses for children are calculated by body weight ([CDC Yellow Book, checked November 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/malaria.html)). A travel health professional will advise on the right option for each child.

The FCDO's Kenya health page also notes that on 6 October 2026 the Kenyan Ministry of Health confirmed its first imported case of Ebola, with screening and contact tracing in place ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/health)). Keep an eye on updates.

On safety, the FCDO advises against all or all but essential travel to several areas, including parts of the north-east, most of Lamu County and a coastal strip between the Tana and Galana rivers ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya)). Check its map against your beach plans. For entry, UK travellers must apply online for an electronic travel authorisation before they travel, ideally at least two weeks ahead ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/kenya/entry-requirements)).

## Mauritius: the easy island

For a beach-only holiday, or the finale to a South African trip, Mauritius is a relaxed choice. TravelHealthPro says there is no risk of malaria in Mauritius, though it lists chikungunya and dengue, both spread by daytime-biting mosquitoes, so repellent still matters ([TravelHealthPro, checked November 2026](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/mauritius)).

The FCDO says UK visitors can stay for up to 60 days without a visa and must complete an online travel form before arrival. If you arrive from a country with malaria risk, such as Kenya, officials may ask for a blood sample ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mauritius/entry-requirements)). It also reported localised demonstrations in October 2026, with temporary evening curfews across most of the main island ([FCDO, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mauritius/safety-and-security)). Check the latest advice before you go.

## Minimum ages, private vehicles and long days

Plenty of safari lodges and activities set minimum ages for game drives, walking safaris and canoeing, and they vary a great deal. Walking safaris in particular are usually reserved for older children and teenagers. Ask before you book, not when you arrive. Some rules are official: Rwanda's tourism board states that the minimum age to track gorillas is 15 ([Visit Rwanda](https://visitrwanda.com/interests/gorilla-tracking/)), so a gorilla trek is one for later years. Our guide to your [first African safari](/stories/first-african-safari/) covers what a typical day looks like.

A private vehicle is often the single best investment for a family. You set the pace, stop for snacks and loo breaks, and head back when attention runs out, without worrying about other guests. South African Tourism's family tips are sensible too: pack snacks and water for game drives, dress children in light, loose clothing and give them a list of animals to tick off ([South African Tourism](https://www.southafrica.net/cd/en/travel/article/a-safari-adventure-for-the-whole-family)).

Keep the transfers short. Two nights in each place is the minimum for most families, and three is better. A day with a four-hour drive should not also include an afternoon game drive.

## Sun, sleep and staying well

The African sun is strong, and children feel it fastest. The NHS advises keeping babies under six months out of direct strong sunlight, keeping children in the shade and covered up especially between 11am and 3pm, and using sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with at least four-star UVA protection, reapplied liberally and often. Wide-brimmed hats that shade the face, neck and ears help too ([NHS, checked November 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/seasonal-health/sunscreen-and-sun-safety/)).

Beyond that, keep a simple first-aid kit, pack plenty of water for long drives and keep the routine as familiar as you can. Book a travel health appointment several weeks before you go, and re-check every health, entry and safety page linked here shortly before you travel, because the advice changes.

Then let the trip do the rest. Somewhere between the penguins and the waterhole, you will notice that nobody has asked for the tablet in days.
